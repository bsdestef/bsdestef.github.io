About Space Explorer

Measuring Space

Space is so big that miles stop being useful. Inside our solar system, astronomers use the astronomical unit (AU), the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 93 million miles. Beyond that, they use the light-year: the distance light travels in one year, about 5.88 trillion miles. Light moves at roughly 186,000 miles per second, but even at that speed, light from distant galaxies takes millions of years to reach us. That means when we look at far-away objects, we are seeing them as they were in the past.

How We See the Universe

Different telescopes see different kinds of light. The Hubble Space Telescope sees visible light, much like our eyes. The James Webb Space Telescope sees infrared light, which passes through clouds of dust that block visible light. X-ray telescopes like Chandra and XMM-Newton detect the extremely hot gas swirling around black holes. Radio telescopes around the world can even be linked together to act like one telescope the size of Earth.

Missions That Changed Astronomy

  • Voyager 1 and 2 (1977): Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus and Neptune.
  • Hubble Space Telescope (1990): has made more than a million observations of the universe.
  • James Webb Space Telescope (2021): the largest space telescope ever launched.
  • Event Horizon Telescope (2019): produced the first image of a black hole.
